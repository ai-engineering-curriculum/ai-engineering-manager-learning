# The Manager's Altitude for Cross-Functional Partnership

An AI engineering team does not ship in isolation. Every feature of consequence passes through a governance review, reads against a product requirements document, consumes capacity from an inference platform the team does not own, and lands in an organisation being simultaneously enabled on AI by a function the manager neither reports to nor controls. The AI Engineering Manager (L45) is the connective tissue across all of that — the single person reading the team's work *out* to governance, legal, security, privacy, product, the AI Infra peer manager, and the AI Workforce siblings, and reading each of those partners' asks *in* to the engineering roadmap.

The word "connective tissue" matters. The manager is not the owner of the governance programme (that is `head-of-ai-governance` (L60) and `senior-ai-governance-architect` (L50)); not the product owner (that is Product); does not run inference infrastructure (that is the peer `ai-infra-team-lead` (L45) on the Infra ladder); does not run org-wide AI enablement (that is the sibling `ai-enablement-lead` (L45) in the AI Workforce family). The manager is the *interface* the AI engineering team exposes to each of these functions, and the interface the team consumes from them. A manager who tries to own any of these partner functions is overstepping; a manager who refuses to interface with them is, in practice, running the team under water.

The rest of the track built the engineering surface the team operates on. Mod-103 taught the roadmap under non-determinism; mod-104 the eval-gated SDLC and release-gate policy; mod-106 the release triad — eval evidence, data posture, safety case — and the signature that clears a GA release; mod-107 incident response and the Article 73-adjacent regulator-facing narrative; mod-110 the budget, FinOps, and build-vs-buy surface, including the Finance and Procurement partnership mechanics. This module is the *integration layer* on top of all of them. It does not re-teach the gate, the incident, the budget, or the safety case. It teaches the partnership interface that makes each of them land.

## Motivation — six failure shapes when the partnership interface has no manager-owned surface

A team can ship competently, clear the engineering gate, close the eval loop, keep the FinOps levers moving, and still misfire on the cross-functional surface in six characteristic ways. None of these are incidents in the mod-107 sense — nothing crashed on the day — but each costs the team credibility with a partner, and most compound. Over a year, a team that trips three of them is a team whose next launch is slower, whose next budget is contested, and whose next incident is responded to by a partner who is not ready.

- **The release that cleared engineering gate but had no governance sign-off.** The team's release triad (mod-106) signed the launch — eval report green, data-flow diagram current, safety case defensible. The feature went live on the Thursday. On the Friday, `senior-ai-governance-architect` (L50) asked to see the artefacts and discovered that the model card and the red-team coverage note had never been reviewed by the governance programme because nobody had asked. At best a precedent-setting policy violation requiring retroactive sign-off; at worst a regulator-adjacent exposure the team did not know it had. The engineering gate was real; it was not the *only* gate. The manager had not drawn the release-approval workflow end-to-end.
- **The PRD that assumed deterministic behaviour and the manager said yes.** The product manager wrote the specification the way a classical feature is specified — "the feature classifies the input and returns one of four responses; here is the acceptance criterion." The AI engineering manager signed off in a meeting and the team spent six weeks delivering against it. In week seven, the eval suite showed the classifier was 86% on the hard subset, not the implicit 100% the acceptance criterion assumed, and the PRD had no language for *what to do when the model is right 86% of the time*. Both the engineering–product and product–stakeholder conversations should have happened before the engineering work began. Mod-103 taught the roadmap under non-determinism; this is what happens when that teaching has not landed with product and the manager has not pushed it.
- **The GPU shortage that surprised the manager because Infra was treated as a ticket queue, not a partner.** The next feature required a model class with heavier per-request inference cost and a larger GPU memory footprint. Engineering built against that assumption. Two weeks before launch, `ai-infra-team-lead` (L45) asked in a planning review how the team was thinking about capacity for the new model; the answer was "we assumed you had it." The platform's capacity plan for the quarter had been written months earlier against the old model class, and no one from engineering had been in the room. Launch slips while Infra provisions. Not Infra's failure; the manager's failure to run shared capacity planning with the Infra peer.
- **The DPIA that counsel asked for the week of launch.** The feature touches at least one privacy trigger — a new processing purpose, a new category of personal data, a new jurisdiction. The manager knew, diffusely, that privacy review would be needed. Nobody formally opened it with the Data Protection Officer or counsel until days before launch, at which point counsel asked for the DPIA inputs — the data-flow diagram, the lawful basis analysis, the risk register — and the team did not have them in a reviewable form. Launch slips; counsel was treated as an escalation rather than as a planned sign-off partner. The manager had not sequenced the privacy review against the release-approval workflow.
- **The CoE enablement rollout that competed with the engineering roadmap because the enablement-lead was not engaged.** `ai-enablement-lead` (L45) rolled out an org-wide AI-productivity initiative — new tooling, new training, new adoption targets — that required every engineering team to participate, including this one. The announcement happened in a function-level forum the AI engineering manager had not attended. The schedule collided with the team's largest release of the quarter. The two rollouts could have been sequenced; neither manager had the interface with the other to notice. Not the enablement-lead's failure; the manager's absence from the sibling-forum where enablement plans are visible before they ship.
- **The legal-opinion-shaped Slack message the manager sent that counsel had to walk back.** A product manager asked in a channel whether the team could use a specific dataset to fine-tune a vendor model. The manager, wanting to unblock, replied: "I think we're fine on that — the DPA allows training on the data because we have customer consent." Plausible, and a legal opinion the manager is not authorised to give. Counsel had to walk it back — clarifying that counsel had not opined, that the consent language was different from the summary, and that a formal review was required. Nothing crashed, but risk was imported: anyone reading the message in isolation is now operating off a legal position the organisation did not sanction. The pattern — *read, flag, escalate, do not opine solo* — is the discipline mod-110 opened and this module reinforces across every sign-off partner.

Each is a *manager-owned partnership surface* failure, not a failure of the function on the other side. Governance did not drop the ball; the manager had not drawn the sign-off workflow. Product did not deliver a bad PRD; the manager had not pushed the PRD shape under non-determinism. Infra did not fail to plan; the manager had not shared the planning input. Counsel did not slow the launch; the manager had not sequenced the review. Enablement did not sabotage the roadmap; the manager was not in the room. The module exists to make each surface a thing the manager actively operates.

## Core concept 1 — the five partnership surfaces

The module builds five partnership surfaces, mapped one-to-one to the chapters, plus a seventh chapter that names what the role does *not* do.

1. **The release-approval surface (chapter 02, with chapters 03 and 07 continuing it).** The sign-off path across governance, legal, security, and privacy for a GA release — who signs on which evidence (eval reports, red-team findings, model cards, data-flow diagrams, DPIA inputs), what the end-to-end timeline looks like, how the engineering-gate from mod-104 and the release-triad from mod-106 compose into the organisation's release-approval workflow.
2. **The AI Governance family consumption surface (chapter 03).** How the manager consumes engineered-risk evidence from `ai-risk-engineer` (L25) and release-assurance / audit-trail evidence from `ai-evaluation-engineer` (L35); how the manager participates in the governance programme owned by `head-of-ai-governance` (L60) and the control library owned by `senior-ai-governance-architect` (L50) without duplicating any of their scopes.
3. **Product collaboration under non-determinism (chapter 04).** The roadmap shape mod-103 taught meets product-shaped roadmaps and stakeholder-forum cadences; the disagree-and-commit framing when the model unlocks a feature later than the PRD planned for; the PRD-shape translation that makes non-deterministic features legible to a classical product organisation.
4. **AI Infra as a customer-partner (chapter 05).** The peer `ai-infra-team-lead` (L45) runs serving, capacity, GPU planning; the AI engineering team is the customer; the relationship is shared SLOs, shared capacity planning, and a shared incident path that composes with the R17 incident response mod-107 opened.
5. **AI Workforce siblings (chapter 06).** `ai-enablement-lead` (L45) runs org-wide enablement — a function-level partnership the AI engineering team is a customer of; `ai-augmented-team-manager` (L40) runs team-level AI adoption and is the sibling-below on rituals, 1:1s, and the team-level operating cadence.

Chapter 07 closes the module on the **four deferrals** — partners who hold standing authority the AI engineering manager explicitly does not: counsel (legal opinion), procurement and finance (contract execution and financial sign-off), IT identity (SSO / IAM implementation), security operations / DFIR (SOC-side investigation). Each has been named in prior modules; this chapter consolidates them and names the four anti-patterns the manager falls into when the deferrals are blurred.

A team has a **manager-owned partnership surface** when the release-approval workflow is drawn end-to-end with every sign-off partner named; each AI Governance family role has a defined consumption interface; the product partner understands the roadmap shape under non-determinism; the Infra peer and the manager hold shared capacity and SLO planning on a named cadence; the AI Workforce siblings are in a visible operating relationship; and the four deferrals are held cleanly without overstepping or disclaiming.

## Core concept 2 — what the IC owns, versus what the manager owns

The split from the rest of the track repeats. The IC owns the artefact at craft level; the manager owns that the artefact *exists*, is routed to the right partner, and the partner's sign-off or consumption is on file. Five rows worth being specific about.

| What the IC owns (craft) | What the manager owns (system) |
|---|---|
| Authoring the model-card content for a specific feature — the intended use, the known failure modes, the eval-coverage note, the training-data posture note. Owned by the `applied-ai-engineer` or the `ai-eval-engineer` depending on the feature shape. | Owning **that** a model card exists for every GA release, that the card is on the release-approval checklist (chapter 02), and that the card has been reviewed by governance before the release triad signs. |
| Debugging an inference-latency regression with the `ai-infra-inference-engineer` (IC, Infra ladder) — reading the traces, isolating the cause (KV-cache thrash, batching misconfiguration, speculative-decoding mis-tune), proposing the fix. | Owning the shared **service-level objective** with `ai-infra-team-lead` (L45, Infra ladder) — its definition, its measurement surface, its breach threshold, its review cadence; owning the capacity-planning conversation that keeps the SLO defensible (chapter 05). |
| Writing the feature's guardrail — the input classifier, the output filter, the HITL escalation path, the tool-use scope. Owned by the `applied-ai-engineer` or the `agentic-ai-engineer`. | **Commissioning** the red-team engagement from `ai-risk-engineer` (L25), routing the findings into the release-gate, and owning that the guardrail's coverage has been adversarially reviewed before GA (chapter 03). |
| Drafting the data-flow diagram for a feature — the data categories, the processing purposes, the storage locations, the retention windows, the cross-border flows, the sub-processor list. Owned by the `applied-ai-engineer` with privacy-engineering input. | Owning **that** the diagram is signed off by privacy — by the Data Protection Officer or the equivalent privacy-engineering role — before the launch date, and that the DPIA inputs are on file for counsel to review (chapter 02 and chapter 07). |
| Proposing a vendor-API change for cost reasons — switching providers for a specific workflow, moving to a different pricing tier, enabling batch endpoints for background tasks. Owned by the IC against the FinOps lever set mod-110 chapter 06 named. | Owning **that** the vendor change goes through the vendor-portfolio review (mod-105) and the procurement sign-off (mod-110 chapter 07) before the switch ships, and that the DPA addendum is current for the new provider (chapter 02 and chapter 07). |

The healthy pattern: *the IC produces the artefact at craft depth; the manager owns the sign-off workflow it lives in, the routing to the partner who consumes it, the evidence trail that the sign-off occurred, and the cadence that keeps the workflow current.* When the manager drifts into the craft column — "I'll write the model card myself; I want to get it done quickly" — the IC feels bypassed and the content is usually thinner than the IC would have produced. When the manager neglects the system column, the team runs the six failures above and each partner's trust in the AI engineering function erodes.

## Core concept 3 — the four partner families and how they differ

Not every partner is the same shape. Treating them as interchangeable is one of the fastest ways to mismanage the surface. Four structural shapes, each with a characteristic product.

- **Sign-off partners — Governance, Legal, Security, Privacy.** They produce **approvals**. The release gate the manager operates sits across their signatures. Each holds standing authority to block a launch, to require an evidence pack, to call for a review. The manager's job is to pre-stage the evidence they need (chapter 02), to sequence the review so it does not land launch week, and to not freelance on their domains — do not draft a legal opinion for counsel to sign; do not draft the SSO configuration for IT identity; do not write the security control for security to accept; do not perform the DPIA for privacy to rubber-stamp.
- **Consumption partners — the AI Governance family (`ai-risk-engineer` (L25), `ai-evaluation-engineer` (L35), `senior-ai-governance-architect` (L50), `head-of-ai-governance` (L60)).** They produce **evidence**. The manager *consumes* their work but does not own their function. The engineered-risk report from the risk-engineer is an input to the release gate; the release-assurance / audit-trail report from the evaluation-engineer is a downstream consumer of the team's eval evidence; the control library from the governance architect is the shape the manager's artefacts must conform to; the governance programme the head-of-ai-governance runs is the standing authority the manager's surface composes with. The failure mode is duplicating any of these functions — running a parallel risk programme, writing a parallel control library, publishing a parallel audit-trail. The right pattern is a clean *consumption interface* (chapter 03): what the manager asks for, what evidence is produced in return, and on what cadence.
- **Collaboration partners — Product.** They **co-plan** the roadmap. This is not a sign-off relationship and not a pure consumption relationship. The engineering roadmap under non-determinism (mod-103) and the product roadmap meet in the middle; the artefact is a jointly-owned sequencing document. Both failure shapes are one-way: a manager who treats product's PRD as an instruction to be executed is under-serving the team; a product organisation that treats engineering as a vendor is under-serving the business. Chapter 04 teaches the co-planning interface, including the disagree-and-commit framing when the model unlocks a feature on a different timeline than the PRD assumed.
- **Organisational peers — AI Infra peer (`ai-infra-team-lead` (L45)) and AI Workforce siblings (`ai-enablement-lead` (L45), `ai-augmented-team-manager` (L40)).** They **coordinate**. Peer-managers in adjacent functions whose work composes with the AI engineering team's. The coordination surface is capacity planning and SLOs with Infra (chapter 05), enablement and adoption with the AI Workforce siblings (chapter 06), and visible operating cadences with both. The failure shape is isolation — a manager with no standing sibling-forum, no scheduled peer-to-peer with the Infra lead, who hears about the enablement rollout from Slack. The right pattern is a small number of durable coordination rituals, each on a cadence, each with a named counterpart.

The pattern to internalise: *sign-off partners produce approvals; consumption partners produce evidence; collaboration partners co-plan; organisational peers coordinate.* The four shapes call for four different cadences, four different artefacts, and four different conversational registers. A manager who runs the governance sign-off conversation the way they run the Infra capacity-planning conversation will mishandle both — governance will feel managed, Infra will feel audited.

## Core concept 4 — a RACI for the four most consequential artefacts

The four shapes are concept-level. In practice the manager is asked, roughly weekly, "who owns this?" and the answer has to be clean. The RACI below names Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed for the four most consequential artefacts across the partnership surface. The table is deliberately conservative — engineering is not A for things engineering does not sign, and the function with standing authority on the artefact is A, not engineering.

| Artefact | Engineering | AI Governance | Legal | Security | Privacy | Product | AI Infra | AI Workforce |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| GA release decision (the signed release-triad decision, including the model-card and data-flow diagram) | R, A for the engineering release note and the eval evidence | C — consumes the release-assurance report (`ai-evaluation-engineer`); may be C on the control-conformance review (`senior-ai-governance-architect`) | C — reviews the DPA posture and any disclosure language | C — reviews the security posture (data handling, prompt / secret exposure, tool-use scope) | C — reviews the data-flow diagram and the DPIA inputs | C — product sign-off on the launch scope and the stakeholder communication | I — informed on the capacity ask and the launch date | I — informed on any user-facing change relevant to adoption |
| Incident Article 73 narrative (the regulator-facing engineering summary; mod-107 chapter 07) | R for the engineering-side facts, timeline, mitigations, impact | C — consumes any governance-side framing | **A** — counsel is accountable for the regulator-facing filing and legal argument | C — on the security-incident framing where applicable; may hand to DFIR for SOC-side pieces | C — on any personal-data exposure | I — informed through counsel's disclosure guidance | C — on the serving-side timeline and the mitigation lever pulled | I — informed on any customer-communication implications |
| New-model portfolio addition (the decision to add a new base model / vendor to the portfolio; mod-105) | R, A for the engineering recommendation and the build-vs-buy matrix (mod-110 chapter 04) | C — the engineered-risk review and the control-conformance read | C — reviews the vendor contract, DPA, and any new jurisdictional posture | C — reviews the data-handling posture of the new vendor | C — reviews the DPA data-processing terms and the sub-processor list | I — informed on the scope of what the new model unlocks | C — reviews the serving-side implications (self-host vs. vendor-API vs. fine-tune) and the capacity ask | I — informed on any enablement implications |
| Team CoE enablement adoption (adopting an org-wide enablement initiative into the team's rituals) | R, A for the team's adoption plan and its sequencing against the engineering roadmap | I — informed if the adoption touches governance-relevant tooling | I — informed if the adoption touches contract-relevant tooling | C — on any new tooling that touches team-side data posture | C — on any new tooling that processes personal data | C — on the sequencing against the product roadmap | I — informed on any serving-side implications of new tooling | **A** — `ai-enablement-lead` (L45) is accountable for the org-wide initiative; the AI engineering manager is R for the team's adoption of it |

Three readings are load-bearing. First: engineering is accountable for the engineering release note and the new-model engineering recommendation, but **not** for the regulator-facing Article 73 narrative — counsel is, and the manager co-drafts the engineering summary that counsel's filing reads off (mod-107 chapter 07). Second: engineering is **not** accountable for the DPIA, the DPA review, the SSO configuration, or the security-incident SOC-side investigation — each has a named partner with standing authority. Third: on the CoE adoption row, the org-wide initiative is accountable to the enablement-lead; the AI engineering manager owns the team's local adoption and its sequencing, not the initiative itself. A manager who finds themselves marked A on an artefact outside the engineering column should notice and route it — *read, flag, escalate, do not opine solo* repeats here.

## What good looks like

A team has a manager-owned partnership surface when:

- The release-approval workflow is drawn end-to-end on a single page — every sign-off partner, every evidence artefact, every hand-off timing named — and the current GA release has a dated trail of each sign-off.
- The AI Governance family consumption interface is explicit: red-team engagements are commissioned from `ai-risk-engineer` (L25) on a named cadence; the release-assurance / audit-trail pack goes to `ai-evaluation-engineer` (L35) on a named cadence; the manager has read the current control library from `senior-ai-governance-architect` (L50) and knows which controls apply to the current roadmap.
- The product partner has an operating PRD template that accommodates non-deterministic features — a quality band, a failure-mode section, a HITL escalation path — and the co-planning cadence (mod-103 met with product's) is scheduled, not ad hoc.
- A shared SLO is on file with `ai-infra-team-lead` (L45); the shared capacity-planning conversation is on a quarterly cadence; the current quarter's capacity commitment is known in writing.
- The AI Workforce siblings are in a visible operating relationship — a standing sibling-forum with the enablement-lead and a sibling-below cadence with `ai-augmented-team-manager` (L40); the enablement initiatives the team will adopt this quarter are known and sequenced against the roadmap.
- The four deferrals are held cleanly — counsel sees DPA-adjacent language before the manager publishes a position; procurement sees vendor-change requests through the portfolio-review path (mod-110); IT identity owns SSO / IAM; security operations / DFIR owns the SOC-side investigation on security-shaped incidents.
- The last two GA releases cleared the workflow without a last-week escalation; the last incident (mod-107) produced a regulator-facing engineering summary counsel could use without rewriting.

## What bad looks like

- The release-approval workflow is not drawn; each launch's sign-off is re-discovered by Slack; one of the sign-off partners is routinely surprised by the launch date.
- AI Governance family relationships are absent or implicit — nobody has commissioned a red-team engagement this year; the team does not know which release-assurance / audit-trail evidence the evaluation-engineer consumes; the current control library has never been opened.
- Product PRDs are written as if the model were deterministic; the team is silently re-translating every PRD into a non-deterministic shape and losing the translation in Slack threads; disagree-and-commit is treated as a conflict event, not a routine one.
- The relationship with the Infra peer is a ticket queue — tickets go out when something breaks, and capacity planning for next quarter is a surprise on both sides.
- The AI Workforce siblings are strangers — the manager hears about the enablement rollout from Slack; `ai-augmented-team-manager` (L40) is a name in an org chart; nothing is co-sequenced.
- The manager has drafted a legal opinion in Slack, a security configuration in a wiki, or an identity-management decision in a release note that a deferral partner later had to walk back.
- The team's credibility with at least one sign-off partner is visibly low; the next launch is routed through an extra review layer previous launches did not need; the explanation is "we had to be sure this time."

## Summary

- The AI Engineering Manager (L45) is the connective tissue between the governance programme, the product organisation, security / privacy / legal partners, the AI Infra peer manager, and the AI Workforce siblings. The role does not own any of those functions — it is the interface the team exposes to and consumes from them.
- The module builds five partnership surfaces (release-approval, AI Governance consumption, Product collaboration under non-determinism, AI Infra as customer-partner, AI Workforce siblings) and one chapter of deferrals (counsel, procurement / finance, IT identity, security operations / DFIR). The release-approval surface opens in chapter 02 and threads through chapters 03 and 07.
- Partners come in four structural shapes: sign-off partners produce approvals; consumption partners produce evidence; collaboration partners co-plan; organisational peers coordinate. Each calls for a different cadence, artefact, and register.
- The RACI on the four most consequential artefacts is deliberately conservative: engineering is accountable for the engineering release note and the new-model engineering recommendation; counsel is accountable for the regulator-facing Article 73 narrative; the enablement-lead is accountable for the org-wide enablement initiative; engineering is R, not A, for its local adoption.
- This module does not re-teach the eval gate (mod-104), the release triad (mod-106), incident response (mod-107), the budget surface (mod-110), or the safety case. It teaches the *interface* between the AI engineering team and each partner that makes those surfaces land.

The next chapter opens the first surface: the release-approval workflow end-to-end, the evidence pack each sign-off partner reads, and the timeline that keeps the review out of launch week.
