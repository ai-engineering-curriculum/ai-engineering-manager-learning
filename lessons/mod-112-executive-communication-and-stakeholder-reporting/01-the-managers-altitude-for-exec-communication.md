# The Manager's Altitude for Executive Communication and Stakeholder Reporting

An AI engineering team at level 45 does not report to a leadership audience that reads eval reports. The VP who approves the quarterly spend sees three slides a quarter; the SVP who sits on the AI steering committee sees one slide a month; the board or external stakeholder sees one paragraph twice a year. Everything the team did — the twenty prompts rewritten, the three evals stood up, the one vendor swap, the one P2 incident, the two features that missed their eval delta and the one that beat it — is compressed by the manager into a reporting surface leadership can actually read.

The compression step is the job. A manager who hands leadership an eval-report attachment and calls it "reporting" has not reported; they have delegated the compression upward to people whose job is not to decode AI-engineering artefacts. A manager who hands leadership a slide of hits with the misses quietly absent has also not reported; they have marketed. This module is about the narrow middle: compression that is honest, legible, and compounds across quarters.

The rest of the track built the engineering surface the reporting reads *on*. Mod-103 taught the roadmap under non-determinism; mod-104 the eval-gated SDLC and release-gate policy; mod-106 the release triad; mod-107 the incident response and its regulator-facing narrative; mod-108 performance; mod-109 the operating cadence; mod-110 the budget and FinOps artefacts; mod-111 the cross-functional partnership surface, including the governance, product, legal, security, privacy and infra partners whose sign-offs the reporting references. This module is the *stakeholder-facing* layer on top of all of them. It does not re-teach the gate, the incident, the budget, or the partnership. It teaches the reporting discipline that makes each of those surfaces legible to leadership and defensible against scrutiny.

## Motivation — six failure shapes when the reporting surface has no manager-owned discipline

A team can ship competently, clear the gate, close the loop, pull the FinOps levers, run the partnership surface, and still lose altitude with leadership in six characteristic ways. None of these show up in the engineering postmortem; each erodes trust slowly and shows up as a thinner budget in the next planning cycle.

- **The weekly that reads like a status-of-tickets report.** The manager sends a weekly to the director: six bullets of "shipped X, started Y, Z is blocked on vendor." No read on the week's eval scores; no read on the week's trace sample; no read on the quarter's release-gate posture; no read on the model-uncertainty state. A director who inherits this weekly for three months has no idea whether the AI feature is getting better, getting worse, or holding; they will ask in the fourth month and the answer will take the manager two days to assemble. The weekly was being produced; it was not reading on any AI-specific surface.
- **The investment ask that referenced "industry productivity gains" in the abstract.** The manager asks for two headcount and double the labelling-data budget. The slide references "industry research on AI productivity" without naming the paper, without naming the population, without naming the conditions under which the gains applied. The CFO asks "which study, which population, which conditions" and the manager cannot answer. The ask is not denied; it is deferred, "come back when you have the evidence." By the time the manager returns, the quarter has moved and the headcount slot is gone.
- **The regression that was reported only once the Slack thread forced it.** The team shipped a release that cleared the release gate but regressed on a specific user-segment eval slice. The miss was known at launch day; nobody surfaced it in the next weekly, nor in the monthly, nor in the quarterly. Six weeks later a customer-success lead flagged the regression in a cross-functional Slack and the director saw it for the first time. The team's credibility is now downgraded, not because the regression was unusual — regressions are routine — but because the team was *perceived to have hidden it*. The regression discipline the team practises internally (mod-107 blameless postmortems, mod-104 trace review) did not propagate upward because the manager did not have a routine that published the miss.
- **The demo that ran away from the eval story.** The team's product director asked for a demo to show the CEO. The IC who built the feature prepared a demo against the three hero prompts the feature handles well. The CEO was impressed, authorised a new cross-team rollout, and the rollout then hit the eval story the demo had walked past — a jagged frontier where 30% of real prompts land on failure modes the hero prompts did not exhibit. The team is now committed to a rollout they cannot staff and cannot evaluate. The manager was in the room; the manager let the demo happen.
- **The external talk that had to be walked back.** A senior engineer gave a conference talk citing "our feature's 94% accuracy" from the hero slice of the eval report. The full-suite number is 71% with a long tail of harder failures. Twitter noticed; the talk was reframed in a blog post the next week; the team's external credibility took a hit the manager had to repair in the next all-hands. Not a bad talk; a demo-discipline failure — the team had no internal review of external-facing claims before they left the building.
- **The next-step conversation that never happened.** The manager has been at level 45 for three years. The role at the next altitude — director of AI engineering, head of AI engineering, chief AI officer at a smaller org — requires a different artefact portfolio than they have been accumulating. The manager's calibration has been "doing the job well"; it has not been "building the artefacts the next role will read against." Three years later they are passed over for a promotion they cannot articulate a case for; the deliberate-practice plan was never written. This is a reporting failure too — the manager was not reading on their own career surface with the discipline they apply to the team's.

Each is a *manager-owned reporting surface* failure, not a failure of leadership to read carefully or of the team to produce the evidence. The eval scores were there; the misses were there; the industry research exists; the demo could have been reviewed; the next-step pivots are well documented across the industry. The manager did not operate the reporting discipline that would have surfaced each.

## Core concept 1 — three cadences, three audiences, three compression ratios

The reporting surface is not one artefact. It is three, each tuned to a cadence, an audience, and a compression ratio.

1. **The weekly team roll-up.** Audience: the manager's direct line (an engineering director or VP) and the horizontal partners (product counterpart, infra peer, governance peer). Compression: ~500 words, 5 minutes to read. Purpose: situational awareness. The roll-up reads on the week's engineering surface (release-gate posture, eval-score trajectory, trace sample, provider surface, open incidents) so partners can make decisions without a sync. Chapter 02 is the depth.
2. **The monthly deep-dive.** Audience: the manager's director, the second-line (VP or SVP), and the cross-functional leaders who read AI engineering as a shared surface. Compression: ~2 pages, 15 minutes to read. Purpose: trend reading and decision prompts. The deep-dive reads on month-over-month trajectory of the headline eval metrics, the model-portfolio posture, the budget-and-usage line, the incident surface, the next month's planned releases. Chapter 02 covers it.
3. **The quarterly exec-ready summary.** Audience: the executive altitude (VP, SVP, CAIO, possibly CTO or CEO) and, through them, the board or the external narrative. Compression: ~1 page or 3 slides, 5 minutes to read. Purpose: altitude-level narrative and investment-ask setup. The quarterly carries the quarter's shipped outcomes, the honest miss ledger, the model-uncertainty posture at quarter-end, the quarter's investment reading, and the next quarter's planned bets. Chapter 02 teaches it.

The three cadences are not independent; the weekly's eval-score trajectory feeds the monthly's trend read, which feeds the quarterly's narrative. A manager who writes each cadence from scratch is doing three times the work and inevitably contradicts themselves across the cadences. The healthy pattern is a **single source of truth** — a team handbook page or a dashboard — that the three cadences read off at different compression ratios.

The three audiences are not the same reader. The weekly's reader will tolerate jargon (eval delta, judge drift, red-team pass) because the partners it serves share the vocabulary. The quarterly's reader will not — "the model-under-test regressed 4.1 points on the internal-support eval, within the 5-point release-gate allowance" has to translate to "the feature held quality against the release gate, inside tolerance, with the specific miss noted and already queued for the next release." The translation is the manager's job; it is not a weakness of the executive reader.

## Core concept 2 — the honesty-by-construction discipline

A reporting cadence is only as good as the honesty discipline that structures it. The reporting that bends toward optimism — or toward pessimism, which is a rarer but real failure — degrades the signal leadership uses to allocate capital, approve releases, and set the external narrative. The honesty discipline this module teaches has four moves, each of which structures a specific artefact in the chapters ahead.

- **The paired hits / misses ledger (chapter 04).** For every release shipped, the manager publishes the hit *and* the miss on the same artefact, with the reason for each. A release with no miss worth naming is suspicious — either the eval surface is not covering the failure modes it should, or the release was too small to generate a signal. A release with only hits is a marketing artefact, not a reporting one.
- **The confidence-band ROI (chapter 03, composing mod-110 chapter 03).** Investment asks that project a single point-estimate ROI are dishonest under the non-determinism of AI outputs. The three-band low / mid / high projection with named assumptions, and the honest acknowledgement that the low band might come in below the ask's cost, is the only defensible shape. A manager who publishes point estimates is not being punished *on this quarter's ask*; they are losing credibility against the *next* ask, when the actual outcome lands between the bands and leadership has no grammar to read it.
- **The cited evidence against the generic claim (chapter 03).** Empirical productivity research on AI engineering exists — *Dell'Acqua et al., "Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier," HBS Working Paper 24-013, 2023* and *Peng et al., "The Impact of AI on Developer Productivity: Evidence from GitHub Copilot," arXiv:2302.06590, 2023* are the two most-cited studies, with real effect sizes measured under specific conditions. The honest ask cites the study by name, names the population and the condition, and reads the team's specific context *against* the study's generalisability window. The dishonest ask says "industry research shows significant productivity gains" and attaches no citation. Chapter 03 is specific about the citation discipline.
- **The demo-review checklist (chapter 05).** The cherry-picked demo is the single most-recurrent failure in AI-engineering stakeholder communication. The team's internal demo discipline — the three hero prompts, the three hard prompts, the two prompts the team expects to fail — is the fix. Every external-facing demo, slide deck, or talk the team produces passes a manager-chaired checklist before it ships. Chapter 05 is the depth.

These four moves are not optional dressing on an otherwise-permissive reporting posture. They are the construction. The manager who skips them is building a reporting surface that will not survive three quarters of scrutiny.

## Core concept 3 — what the IC owns, what the manager owns

The split from the rest of the track repeats here. The IC owns the artefact at craft depth; the manager owns the *compression* of the artefact into the reporting surface, the *routing* of the reporting to the right audience, and the *honesty discipline* that structures each cadence.

| What the IC owns (craft) | What the manager owns (system) |
|---|---|
| Writing the week's eval-score note on the trace-review (mod-104, chapter 06). The specific regressions seen on the specific slices; the specific fixtures raised; the specific judges that drifted. | Compressing the eval-score note into the weekly roll-up's `eval` line: three numbers, the delta vs. last week, the drift note if any, the sentence of context that lets the director read it without opening the trace-review doc (chapter 02). |
| Drafting the postmortem R16 for a specific incident (mod-107, chapter 05). The timeline, the contributing factors, the engineering-side mitigations, the fixture additions, the follow-up actions with owners. | Compressing the postmortem into the monthly deep-dive's incident line and the quarterly's miss ledger — and owning that the miss is *published* at each cadence, not quietly absorbed (chapter 04). |
| Running the demo for the CEO. Picking which prompts to show; executing against the live system; narrating the trade-offs. | Reviewing the demo set against the demo-discipline checklist (chapter 05); owning that the hero prompts and the hard prompts are both represented; chairing the pre-brief that sets the CEO's expectations before the demo begins. |
| Producing the eval-cost reading for the manager's monthly deep-dive — the per-feature spend, the caching rate, the lever being pulled this month (mod-110 chapter 06). | Translating the eval-cost reading into the monthly's `budget` line: the one sentence of trajectory, the one sentence of the lever being pulled, the line-item that reads back to Finance's chart of accounts (chapter 02). |
| Giving the conference talk; writing the blog post; being on the podcast. | Reviewing the external-facing claim against the full-suite eval data (chapter 05); owning that the internal-review step happened before the artefact left the building. |

The healthy pattern: *the IC produces the artefact at craft depth; the manager compresses it to the right altitude for each cadence, publishes it on the cadence's schedule, and ensures the honesty moves are made at each publication.* When the manager drifts into the craft column — rewriting the eval note themselves, running the demo themselves — the IC's authorship is diluted and the reporting is thinner than it should be. When the manager neglects the compression column, the reporting either does not happen or lands at the wrong altitude.

## Core concept 4 — what this module does not re-teach

Everything on the shortlist below is assumed. If the reader has not internalised it, the referral is prior modules in this track or the level-40 sibling (`ai-augmented-team-manager`).

- **General exec communication canon.** Scott Berkun's *Making Things Happen*, Barbara Minto's *The Pyramid Principle*, the executive-communication chapters of *The Manager's Path* (Fournier), Lara Hogan's *Resilient Management* chapters on communicating up. This module uses the canon and specialises it; it does not re-derive it. The level-40 sibling teaches the general mechanics.
- **The eval-gated SDLC.** Mod-104 owns the registry, the thresholds, the drift shapes, the trace review. The weekly roll-up reads *on* the mod-104 artefacts; it does not re-build them.
- **Incident response and postmortems.** Mod-107 owns the R16 postmortem, the Article 73 regulator-facing narrative, the incident corpora. The miss-ledger discipline in chapter 04 of this module *reads from* the postmortem; it does not re-author it.
- **The budget-and-economics surface.** Mod-110 owns the cost model, the ROI projection with confidence bands, the build-vs-buy matrix, the quarterly portfolio allocation, the FinOps levers. The investment-ask narrative in chapter 03 of this module *composes* those artefacts into a leadership-facing ask; it does not re-build them.
- **The cross-functional partnership surface.** Mod-111 owns the release-approval workflow and the governance / legal / security / privacy sign-offs. The weekly and monthly reporting *reads on* those sign-offs; it does not re-teach the workflow.
- **Performance management.** Mod-108 owns the IC's growth plan, the artefact-class grid, the calibration cycle. The next-step chapter in this module (chapter 06) *applies the same growth-plan discipline to the manager's own career surface*; it does not re-teach the IC-side mechanics.

If any of the above are unfamiliar, read them before reading this module. Everything here assumes those mechanics are in the manager's hands and asks: *given those mechanics, how do you specialise exec communication to the AI-engineering surface honestly?*

## What good looks like

A team has a manager-owned reporting surface when:

- A written weekly roll-up is produced on a fixed day; the format is stable across quarters; the director and partners can read it in under five minutes and know what moved.
- A monthly deep-dive is produced on a fixed day; the eval-score trajectory, the budget trajectory, and the incident surface are all read month-over-month against a stable baseline.
- A quarterly exec-ready summary is produced in the first week of the quarter close; the quarterly hits and misses are both present; the next quarter's bets are legible as bets with named confidence bands.
- The investment ask is dated; references cited productivity research by name with the generalisability window stated; carries three confidence bands with named assumptions; is read against the team's specific context.
- Regressions are published at the cadence they emerge — a release miss shows up in the next weekly, the next monthly's rollup, and the quarter-end miss ledger — with the reason named, not just the fact of the miss.
- Every external-facing artefact (demo, slide, blog, talk) passes the demo-discipline checklist before it ships; a named reviewer signs off; the sign-off is on file.
- The manager has a written next-step plan with a named target role, a dated artefact portfolio, and a deliberate-practice loop.

## What bad looks like

- The weekly is a status-of-tickets report; the director's read is "what shipped" without a model-uncertainty or eval-gate posture.
- The monthly is a longer version of the weekly; no trend reading; no decision prompt.
- The quarterly surfaces only hits; the misses are either absent or buried in an appendix.
- The investment ask references "industry productivity research" without naming the study; the point-estimate ROI is a single number; the CFO defers the ask for lack of evidence.
- Regressions are published only when a partner forces them; the team's credibility erodes quarter by quarter and the next launch is routed through an extra review layer.
- External demos run from hero prompts; the first external artefact of the quarter cites a number the full-suite eval does not support; a walk-back is required.
- The manager has no written next-step plan; the calibration conversation at year-end reads "doing the job well" and nothing has compounded.

## Summary

- The AI Engineering Manager (L45) compresses the team's engineering surface into three reporting cadences — weekly, monthly, quarterly — for three audiences at three compression ratios. The compression is the job; delegating it upward loses signal.
- Honesty is a construction choice: the paired hits-and-misses ledger, the confidence-band projection, the cited evidence, and the demo-discipline checklist are the four moves that keep the reporting defensible across quarters.
- The IC produces the artefact at craft depth; the manager owns the compression, the routing, and the honesty discipline that structures each cadence.
- This module does not re-teach the eval gate (mod-104), the incident response (mod-107), the budget surface (mod-110), the partnership surface (mod-111), or the general exec-communication canon (level-40 sibling and *The Pyramid Principle*). It teaches the specialisation that makes each of those legible to leadership honestly.
